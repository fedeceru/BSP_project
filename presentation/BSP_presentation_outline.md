## Slide 2 — Clinical motivation

**On-slide content:**
- Fetal cardiac activity → key indicator of fetal well-being
- Doppler ultrasound: routine, but gives *only* heart rate (FHR), not morphology
- Fetal ECG (FECG) also carries PR/QRS/ST morphology → linked to fetal oxygenation
- Abdominal FECG = non-invasive, usable throughout pregnancy *and* labour (vs. invasive scalp electrode, labour-only)

**Image:** none (optionally the ECG nomenclature figure from the paper, fig. 1, if allowed).

**Speaker notes:** Frame why this matters clinically: Doppler ultrasound is the current standard but only gives FHR; abdominal FECG could add morphological information non-invasively, and — unlike a scalp electrode — from early pregnancy through labour, and even for long-term ambulatory/home monitoring.

## Slide 3 — The core challenge

**On-slide content:**
- FECG amplitude: tiny (tens of µV)
- Buried under: baseline wander, power-line interference, EMG/motion artifacts
- Above all: the **mother's ECG (MECG)** — an order of magnitude larger, same electrodes
- Goal: strip away everything that isn't the fetal heartbeat

**Image:** none (a cropped S1-column teaser from `results/sequential_analysis_pipeline.png` was considered, but precisely cropping a matplotlib subplot boundary proved unreliable — the same figure appears in full on Slide 9 anyway).

**Speaker notes:** Set up the signal-processing problem: on an abdominal recording, the fetal signal is small and mixed with several much larger interferers, dominated by the mother's own ECG. Every stage of the pipeline exists to remove one of these specific interferers.

---

## Slide 4 — Two paradigms

**On-slide content:**
- **Blind (ICA / BSS):** no prior knowledge — just statistical independence of sources
- **Non-blind (sequential analysis):** exploits *a priori* knowledge of each interferer (periodicity, morphology, frequency content)
- Paper's thesis: the non-blind approach should be **more robust**, especially at low SNR
- → This is what we test on real data in this presentation

**Image:** none — a simple two-box comparison diagram.

**Speaker notes:** Two philosophically different approaches exist in the literature. ICA treats the problem as pure blind source separation. The paper instead proposes exploiting what we *already know* about each interference source — its periodicity, its expected morphology — to remove them one by one. The rest of the talk tests whether that pays off.

## Slide 6 — Stage 1–2 in practice: baseline wander + power-line interference

**On-slide content:**
- Baseline wander: linear-phase FIR high-pass, 3 Hz cutoff, Hamming window — **matches the paper exactly**
- Power-line interference: adaptive PLL-style canceller with a QRS-blocking mask (protects heartbeats while tracking the 50 Hz line) — same adaptive-cancellation spirit as the paper, different mechanism
- Result: 50 Hz line essentially gone from the spectrum

**Image:** `results/baseline_wander_removal_power_line_interference_cancellation_s1_s3.png` (primary); `results/baseline_wander_remover_fir_filter_stage_1.png` (backup — the FIR filter's own magnitude/phase response)

**Speaker notes:** The baseline wander filter is a direct, faithful implementation of the paper's design — if asked to justify the filter design itself, the backup transfer-function plot shows the expected flat 0 dB passband and a sharp roll-off right at the 3 Hz cutoff, with a clean linear phase confirming no distortion. Power-line cancellation uses a different adaptive mechanism (phase-locked-loop-style tracking with a mask that avoids distorting QRS complexes) but achieves the same goal. Point to the PSD panel on the right of the main image — the 50 Hz peak is clearly gone after processing.

## Slide 7 — Stage 3–4 in practice: maternal QRS detection & MECG cancellation

**On-slide content:**
- Maternal QRS detection: PCA channel-combination (maximises SNR) + cross-correlation matched filter — **matches the paper**
- MECG cancellation: average P-wave/QRS/T-wave templates, each **scaled independently** via least squares (paper's method)
- This implementation adds **Ridge (Tikhonov) regularisation** to that least-squares fit for numerical stability — one deviation from the original paper

**Image:** `results/maternal_qrs_detection_and_mecg_cancellation_s4_s5.png`

**Speaker notes:** QRS localisation follows the paper closely: combine channels via PCA to boost SNR, then find peaks by matched filtering. MECG removal also follows the paper's core idea — average and independently rescale the P, QRS and T segments to account for morphology changing over time — but this implementation regularises the least-squares fit (Ridge/Tikhonov) for stability, an addition not in the original paper. The image shows the large maternal complexes (S4) almost entirely gone after cancellation (S5).

---

## Slide 8 — Stage 5 in practice: fetal QRS detection & FECG extraction

**On-slide content:**
- PCA enhancement of the MECG-free signal
- Candidate peaks: squared-signal **energy thresholding**
- Refinement: cross-correlation matched filter
- Final step: synchronous averaging of ~150 beats → SNR boost of √N (paper's approach)

**Image:** right half of `results/maternal_and_fetal_qrs_detection.png`

**Speaker notes:** With the MECG gone, the much smaller fetal QRS complexes are found the same way as the maternal ones were, but on the energy of the squared signal rather than the raw amplitude, then confirmed by matched filtering. Averaging many detected beats together — as the paper does — sharply improves the effective SNR of the extracted FECG template.

## Slide 12 — Evaluation metrics

**On-slide content:**
- Success = ≥60% of the recording covered by physiologically valid, RR-interval-derived FHR estimates
- Reliability = 1 − (outliers / total), outlier = >10 bpm deviation from the 10s-block median FHR
- SNRⱼ, SIRⱼ (dB) — from S6 (averaged FECG) vs. S4−S5 (MECG) and vs. residual noise power
- Precision (PPV) = TP / (TP+FP), Recall (Sensitivity) = TP / (TP+FN), F1 — matched to `.fqrs` ground truth at ±50 ms tolerance
- FHR-value MAE / RMSE (bpm) — estimated vs. ground-truth FHR, interpolated onto a common 1 s grid

**Image:** none (formulas only — keep this slide text-light, e.g. large equation typesetting).

**Speaker notes:** These are exactly the metrics computed in this codebase. Success and reliability follow the paper's definitions; SNR/SIR are derived per-channel from the pipeline's own intermediate signals; precision/recall/F1 come from directly matching detected peaks to the dataset's ground-truth fetal QRS annotations within a 50 ms window; MAE/RMSE instead compare the actual FHR *values* (bpm) of the two traces once interpolated onto a shared time grid — a complementary check on top of precision/recall/F1, since a method can match peak locations well but still drift on the derived heart rate, or vice versa. There's no separate classification-style "accuracy" metric here — precision/recall/F1 together with MAE/RMSE are more informative for a detection-plus-estimation task like this.

## Slide 13 — Results I: success rate & reliability

**On-slide content:**
- FHR detection success rate: **Sequential Analysis 92% vs. ICA 96%** (23/25 vs. 24/25 records)
- Mean FHR reliability (feasible records only): **Sequential Analysis 0.753 vs. ICA 0.684**
- Box plots: SA's spread is tighter and more consistent — ICA is bimodal (several near-perfect records, several collapsed as low as ~0.23)

**Image:** `results/performance_summary.png`

**Speaker notes:** Success rate alone slightly favors ICA here (96% vs. 92% — it produces a passable trace on one more record than SA does). Reliability tells a different, more decisive story: on the records where each method *does* produce a trace, SA's is markedly more reliable on average (0.753 vs 0.684). The box plot shows why — ICA's median is actually comparable to SA's, but its distribution is bimodal: several records are detected almost perfectly, while others collapse to very low reliability (down to about 0.23), which is what pulls its mean down and its spread wide. SA sits more consistently in the mid-to-high range across records. This is the first hint that the full picture is more nuanced than any single metric — the ground-truth comparison on the next slide settles it more decisively.

## Slide 17 — Results V: signal quality drives reliability

**On-slide content:**
- Dataset is genuinely hard: mean SNR ≈ **−4.9 dB**, mean SIR ≈ **−12.0 dB**
- Sequential analysis reliability degrades gracefully as SNR/SIR drop; ICA collapses more sharply — mirrors the paper's own finding
- Spearman ρ: SA–SNR **0.87** (p<0.001), SA–SIR **0.50** (p=0.012) — both significant; ICA–SNR **0.10** (p=0.63), ICA–SIR **0.18** (p=0.40) — neither significant

**Image:** `results/snr_sir_vs_reliability.png` (primary — per-record scatter with linear fit); `results/reliability_correlation_matrix.png` (backup — full Spearman correlation matrix)

**Speaker notes:** This scatter grid plots per-record reliability against the estimated SNR and SIR of each recording, for both methods. The sequential analysis method (top row) holds up reasonably well even as SNR/SIR fall, and this is statistically real: its reliability correlates strongly and significantly with both SNR and SIR. ICA (bottom row) is far noisier and drops to very low reliability more often in the same low-SNR region — and its correlation with SNR/SIR, while positive, is weak and not statistically significant at this sample size. This is exactly the robustness gap the original paper reported, now backed by the correlation numbers rather than just the visual trend.

## Slide 18 — Discussion: why non-blind wins, and where it struggles

**On-slide content:**
- ICA assumes as many independent sources as channels — but real recordings have several MECG dipole components *plus* multiple independent EMG/motion sources *plus* the FECG
- Sequential analysis sidesteps this by exploiting known structure (periodicity, morphology) instead of blind independence
- Limitations, honestly: 7/25 records needed short NaN-gap interpolation; a handful of records (a18, a09, a02, a11, a15) show markedly lower recall
- Consistent with the paper's own note: FECG amplitude is reduced around 28–32 weeks gestation (vernix caseosa)

**Image:** none.

**Speaker notes:** ICA's independence assumption is fragile in this setting — there are simply more real interference sources than the 4 available channels can cleanly separate. Sequential analysis avoids that by using explicit domain knowledge at each stage. That said, be candid about limitations: a few records have real data-quality issues (short signal gaps) and a few show much lower recall than the rest, a pattern the original paper also links to gestational-age-dependent FECG amplitude loss.

**On-slide content:**
- Thesis supported on this dataset: non-blind sequential analysis is **both more reliable and more accurate** against ground truth than blind ICA
- Gap is largest precisely where it matters most: low-SNR/SIR recordings
- Practical relevance: viable direction for affordable, non-invasive, potentially home-based fetal monitoring
- Future work: reduced electrode montages, dynamic MECG windows accounting for heart-rate-dependent QT variation, improved fetal-QRS detector for the low-recall records
